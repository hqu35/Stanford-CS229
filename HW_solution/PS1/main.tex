\item \textbf{[40 points] Linear Classifiers (logistic regression and GDA)}

In this problem, we cover two probabilistic linear classifiers we have
covered in class so far. First, a discriminative linear classifier:
logistic regression. Second, a generative linear classifier: Gaussian
discriminant analysis (GDA). Both algorithms find a linear decision
boundary that separates the data into two classes, but make different
assumptions. Our goal in this problem is to get a deeper understanding
of the similarities and differences (and strengths and weaknesses) of
these two algorithms.

For this problem, we will consider two datasets, provided in the
following files:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*.]
    \item \texttt{data/ds1\_\{train,valid\}.csv}
    \item \texttt{data/ds2\_\{train,valid\}.csv}
\end{enumerate}

Each file contains $m$ examples, one example
$(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})$ per row. In particular, the $i$th row contains
columns $x_0^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}$,
$x_1^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}$, and $y^{(i)}\in\{0,1\}$.
In the subproblems that follow, we will investigate using logistic
regression and Gaussian discriminant analysis (GDA) to perform binary
classification on these two datasets.

\item \textbf{[10 points]}
In lecture we saw the average empirical loss for logistic regression:
\[
J(\theta)
=
-\frac{1}{m}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\left[
y^{(i)}
\log\left(h_\theta(x^{(i)})\right)
+
(1-y^{(i)})
\log\left(1-h_\theta(x^{(i)})\right)
\right],
\]
where
\[
y^{(i)}\in\{0,1\},
\qquad
h_\theta(x)=g(\theta^Tx),
\qquad
g(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}.
\]

Find the Hessian $H$ of this function, and show that for any vector
$z$, it holds true that
\[
z^THz\geq 0.
\]

\textit{Hint:}
You may want to start by showing that
\[
\sum_i\sum_j z_i x_i x_j z_j
=
(x^Tz)^2
\geq 0.
\]
Recall also that
\[
g'(z)=g(z)(1-g(z)).
\]

\textit{Remark:}
This is one of the standard ways of showing that the matrix $H$ is
positive semi-definite, written $H\succeq0$.
This implies that $J$ is convex, and has no local minima other than
the global one. If you have some other way of showing $H\succeq0$,
you are also welcome to use your method instead of the one above.
\vspace{5mm}

% Solution to 1(a) will go here.
\noindent\colorbox{yellow}{\textbf{Solution.}}

Let
\[
h_i
:=
h_\theta(x^{(i)})
=
g(\theta^T x^{(i)}).
\]
For the $i$th training example, define
\[
\ell_i(\theta)
=
-
\left[
y^{(i)}\log h_i
+
(1-y^{(i)})\log(1-h_i)
\right].
\]
Then
\[
J(\theta)
=
\frac{1}{m}
\sum_{i=1}^m
\ell_i(\theta).
\]

Since
\[
g'(t)=g(t)(1-g(t)),
\]
we have
\[
\frac{\partial h_i}{\partial\theta_j}
=
h_i(1-h_i)x_j^{(i)}.
\]

Therefore,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial \ell_i}{\partial\theta_j}
&=
-\frac{y^{(i)}}{h_i}
\frac{\partial h_i}{\partial\theta_j}
+
\frac{1-y^{(i)}}{1-h_i}
\frac{\partial h_i}{\partial\theta_j}
\\
&=
-y^{(i)}(1-h_i)x_j^{(i)}
+
(1-y^{(i)})h_i x_j^{(i)}
\\
&=
(h_i-y^{(i)})x_j^{(i)}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Hence,
\[
\frac{\partial J}{\partial\theta_j}
=
\frac1m
\sum_{i=1}^m
(h_i-y^{(i)})x_j^{(i)},
\]
and thus
\[
\nabla J(\theta)
=
\frac1m
\sum_{i=1}^m
(h_i-y^{(i)})x^{(i)}.
\]

Now consider the $(j,k)$ entry of the Hessian:
\[
\begin{aligned}
H_{jk}
&=
\frac{\partial^2J}
{\partial\theta_j\partial\theta_k}
\\
&=
\frac1m
\sum_{i=1}^m
\frac{\partial h_i}{\partial\theta_k}
x_j^{(i)}
\\
&=
\frac1m
\sum_{i=1}^m
h_i(1-h_i)
x_j^{(i)}x_k^{(i)}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore,
\[
\boxed{
H
=
\frac1m
\sum_{i=1}^m
h_i(1-h_i)
x^{(i)}(x^{(i)})^T
}.
\]

To show that $H$ is positive semidefinite, let
$z\in\mathbb{R}^n$ be arbitrary. Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
z^THz
&=
\frac1m
\sum_{i=1}^m
h_i(1-h_i)
z^Tx^{(i)}(x^{(i)})^Tz
\\
&=
\frac1m
\sum_{i=1}^m
h_i(1-h_i)
\left((x^{(i)})^Tz\right)^2.
\end{aligned}
\]

Since
\[
0<h_i<1,
\]
we have
\[
h_i(1-h_i)\ge0,
\]
and clearly
\[
\left((x^{(i)})^Tz\right)^2\ge0.
\]
Thus every term in the summation is nonnegative, so
\[
\boxed{
z^THz\ge0
}
\]
for every $z\in\mathbb{R}^n$.

Hence,
\[
\boxed{
H\succeq0
}.
\]

\item \textbf{[5 points]}

Recall that in GDA we model the joint distribution of $(x,y)$ by the
following equations:
\[
p(y)
=
\begin{cases}
\phi, & y=1,\\
1-\phi, & y=0,
\end{cases}
\]
\[
p(x\mid y=0)
=
\frac{1}
{(2\pi)^{n/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}}
\exp\left(
-\frac12(x-\mu_0)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu_0)
\right),
\]
and
\[
p(x\mid y=1)
=
\frac{1}
{(2\pi)^{n/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}}
\exp\left(
-\frac12(x-\mu_1)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu_1)
\right),
\]
where $\phi,\mu_0,\mu_1,$ and $\Sigma$ are the parameters of our model.

Suppose we have already fit $\phi,\mu_0,\mu_1,$ and $\Sigma$, and now
want to predict $y$ given a new point $x$.

To show that GDA results in a classifier that has a linear decision
boundary, show the posterior distribution can be written as
\[
p(y=1\mid x;\phi,\mu_0,\mu_1,\Sigma)
=
\frac{1}
{1+\exp\left(-(\theta^Tx+\theta_0)\right)},
\]
where
\[
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^n,
\qquad
\theta_0\in\mathbb{R}
\]
are appropriate functions of $\phi,\Sigma,\mu_0,$ and $\mu_1$.

% Solution to 1(c) will go here.
\vspace{5mm}
\noindent\colorbox{yellow}{\textbf{Solution.}}

By Bayes' rule,
\[
\begin{aligned}
p(y=1\mid x)
&=
\frac{p(x\mid y=1)P(y=1)}{p(x)}
\\[4pt]
&=
\frac{p(x\mid y=1)P(y=1)}
{p(x\mid y=1)P(y=1)+p(x\mid y=0)P(y=0)}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Substituting the Gaussian densities, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
p(y=1\mid x)
=
\frac{
\phi \exp\left(
-\frac12(x-\mu_1)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu_1)
\right)
}{
\begin{aligned}
&\phi \exp\left(
-\frac12(x-\mu_1)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu_1)
\right)
\\
&+(1-\phi)\exp\left(
-\frac12(x-\mu_0)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu_0)
\right)
\end{aligned}
}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Dividing the numerator and denominator by the numerator,
\[
p(y=1\mid x)
=
\frac{1}{
1+\dfrac{1-\phi}{\phi}
\exp\left(
-\frac12
\left[
(x-\mu_0)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu_0)
-
(x-\mu_1)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu_1)
\right]
\right)
}.
\]

Let $\Omega=\Sigma^{-1}$. Since $\Sigma$ is symmetric,
its inverse $\Omega$ is also symmetric.

Now consider the difference of the quadratic forms:
\[
\begin{aligned}
& (x-\mu_0)^T\Omega(x-\mu_0)
-(x-\mu_1)^T\Omega(x-\mu_1)
\\[4pt]
={}&
(x^T-\mu_0^T)\Omega(x-\mu_0)
-(x^T-\mu_1^T)\Omega(x-\mu_1)
\\[4pt]
={}&
x^T\Omega x
-x^T\Omega\mu_0
-\mu_0^T\Omega x
+\mu_0^T\Omega\mu_0
\\
&-x^T\Omega x
+x^T\Omega\mu_1
+\mu_1^T\Omega x
-\mu_1^T\Omega\mu_1.
\end{aligned}
\]

Using the symmetry of $\Omega$, we have
\[
\mu_i^T\Omega x=x^T\Omega\mu_i.
\]

Hence,
\[
\begin{aligned}
& (x-\mu_0)^T\Omega(x-\mu_0)
-(x-\mu_1)^T\Omega(x-\mu_1)
\\[4pt]
={}&
2x^T\Omega(\mu_1-\mu_0)
+\mu_0^T\Omega\mu_0
-\mu_1^T\Omega\mu_1.
\end{aligned}
\]

Substituting this expression back into the posterior,
\[
\begin{aligned}
p(y=1\mid x)
=
\frac{1}{
1+\dfrac{1-\phi}{\phi}
\exp\left(
-x^T\Omega(\mu_1-\mu_0)
-\frac12\mu_0^T\Omega\mu_0
+\frac12\mu_1^T\Omega\mu_1
\right)
}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Since
\[
\frac{1-\phi}{\phi}
=
\exp\left(-\log\frac{\phi}{1-\phi}\right),
\]
we may rewrite the posterior as
\[
p(y=1\mid x)
=
\frac{1}{
1+\exp\left(
-\left[
x^T\Omega(\mu_1-\mu_0)
+\frac12\mu_0^T\Omega\mu_0
-\frac12\mu_1^T\Omega\mu_1
+\log\frac{\phi}{1-\phi}
\right]
\right)
}.
\]

Therefore, defining
\[
\boxed{
\theta=\Sigma^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_0)
}
\]
and by using the differnece-of-squares identity:
\[
\boxed{
\theta_0
=
\frac12\mu_0^T\Sigma^{-1}\mu_0
-\frac12\mu_1^T\Sigma^{-1}\mu_1
+\log\frac{\phi}{1-\phi},
    = \log\frac{\phi}{1-\phi} - \frac{1}{2}(\mu_0 + \mu_1)^{T} \theta}
\]
we conclude that
\[
\boxed{
p(y=1\mid x)
=
\frac{1}{1+\exp(-(\theta^Tx+\theta_0))}.
}
\]

\hfill$\square$

\item \textbf{[7 points]}

In this problem, we derive the maximum likelihood estimators
for Gaussian Discriminant Analysis (GDA), first in one
dimension and then in arbitrary finite dimensions.

Let
\[
\mathcal{D}
=
\left\{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})\right\}_{i=1}^{m}
\]
be a dataset consisting of $m$ independent observations,
where $x^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and $y^{(i)}\in\{0,1\}$.

Recall that under the GDA model,
\[
p(y^{(i)};\phi)
=
\phi^{y^{(i)}}(1-\phi)^{1-y^{(i)}},
\]
and
\[
X\mid Y=k
\sim
\mathcal{N}(\mu_k,\Sigma),
\qquad k\in\{0,1\}.
\]

The log-likelihood of the observations is
\[
\ell(\phi,\mu_0,\mu_1,\Sigma)
=
\log
\prod_{i=1}^{m}
p(x^{(i)}\mid y^{(i)};\mu_0,\mu_1,\Sigma)
p(y^{(i)};\phi).
\]

You may assume that the dataset contains at least
one positive and one negative example.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{(i) The One-Dimensional Case.}

Suppose first that $n=1$, so that
\[
\mu_0,\mu_1\in\mathbb{R},
\qquad
\Sigma=[\sigma^2],
\qquad
\sigma^2>0.
\]

By expanding the log-likelihood and applying ordinary
differentiation, show that the maximum likelihood
estimators of $\phi$ and $\mu_k$ are given by
\[
\boxed{
\hat{\phi}
=
\frac{1}{m}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\ind{y^{(i)}=1}
}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{
\hat{\mu}_k
=
\frac{
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\ind{y^{(i)}=k}x^{(i)}
}{
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\ind{y^{(i)}=k}
},
\qquad k\in\{0,1\}.
}
\]

Now write $v=\sigma^2$ and define
\[
s
=
\frac{1}{m}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\left(
x^{(i)}-\hat{\mu}_{y^{(i)}}
\right)^2.
\]

Show that, after maximizing over the mean parameters,
the terms in the log-likelihood which depend on $v$
can be expressed as
\[
\ell(v)
=
-\frac{m}{2}
\left(
\log v+\frac{s}{v}
\right)
+\text{constant}.
\]

Assuming $s>0$, determine the value of $v$
which maximizes this expression, and hence prove that
\[
\boxed{
\hat{\sigma}^2
=
\frac{1}{m}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\left(
x^{(i)}-\hat{\mu}_{y^{(i)}}
\right)^2.
}
\]

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{(ii) Generalization to Arbitrary Dimensions.}

Suppose now that $n$ is an arbitrary positive integer,
so that
\[
x^{(i)},\mu_0,\mu_1\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
\qquad
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},
\]
where $\Sigma$ is symmetric positive definite.

By choosing an appropriate change of variable, define the \emph{precision matrix}
\[
\boxed{
K = ? .
}
\]

Show that the log-likelihood can be expressed as
\[
\begin{aligned}
\ell(\phi,\mu_0,\mu_1,K)
={}&
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\left[
y^{(i)}\log\phi
+
(1-y^{(i)})\log(1-\phi)
\right]
\\[4pt]
&+
\frac{m}{2}\log\det K
\\[4pt]
&-
\frac{1}{2}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
(x^{(i)}-\mu_{y^{(i)}})^T
K
(x^{(i)}-\mu_{y^{(i)}})
\\[4pt]
&+\text{constant}.
\end{aligned}
\]

For $k\in\{0,1\}$, define
\[
m_k
=
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\ind{y^{(i)}=k}.
\]

By differentiating the log-likelihood with respect
to the mean vectors, show that
\[
\boxed{
\nabla_{\mu_k}\ell
=
K
\left(
\sum_{i:y^{(i)}=k}x^{(i)}
-
m_k\mu_k
\right).
}
\]

Hence, by applying the first-order optimality
condition, deduce that
\[
\boxed{
\hat{\mu}_k
=
\frac{
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\ind{y^{(i)}=k}x^{(i)}
}{
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\ind{y^{(i)}=k}
},
\qquad k\in\{0,1\}.
}
\]

Verify that these stationary points maximize
the log-likelihood with respect to the
mean parameters.
\vspace{3mm}

Now define the residual vectors
\[
r_i
=
x^{(i)}-\hat{\mu}_{y^{(i)}}
\]
and the scatter matrix
\[
\boxed{
S
=
\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_ir_i^T.
}
\]

\noindent\colorbox{yellow!50}{\textbf{Assume that $S$ is positive definite.}}

By expressing the quadratic terms using
the trace of a matrix, show that the
log-likelihood, as a function of $K$,
can be written as
\[
\boxed{
\ell(K)
=
\frac{m}{2}\log\det K
-
\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(KS)
+\text{constant}.
}
\]

Recall the following matrix differentiation identities:
\[
\nabla_A\operatorname{tr}(AB)
=
B^T,
\]
and, for an invertible matrix $A$,
\[
\nabla_A\det A
=
\det(A)(A^{-1})^T.
\]

By applying the chain rule, deduce that
for a symmetric positive definite matrix $A$,
\[
\boxed{
\nabla_A\log\det A
=
(A^{-1})^T.
}
\]

Hence, by differentiating the log-likelihood
with respect to the precision matrix $K$, show that
\[
\boxed{
\nabla_K\ell
=
\frac{1}{2}
\left(
mK^{-1}-S
\right).
}
\]

By applying the first-order optimality condition,
show that
\[
K^{-1}
=
\frac{1}{m}S.
\]

You may use the fact that $\log\det K$
is strictly concave on the set of symmetric
positive definite matrices.

Explain why this implies that the stationary
point is the unique global maximizer
of the log-likelihood.

Finally, recalling that $K=\Sigma^{-1}$,
deduce that
\[
\boxed{
\hat{\Sigma}
=
\frac{1}{m}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\left(
x^{(i)}-\hat{\mu}_{y^{(i)}}
\right)
\left(
x^{(i)}-\hat{\mu}_{y^{(i)}}
\right)^T.
}
\]

Conclude that the maximum likelihood estimators
obtained in part (i) extend to arbitrary finite
dimensions, provided that the empirical residual
covariance matrix is positive definite.

\medskip
\noindent\colorbox{yellow}{\textbf{Solution.}}

\medskip
\noindent\colorbox{yellow!20}{\textbf{(i) The One-Dimensional Case.}}

Suppose that $n=1$, so that
$\mu_0,\mu_1\in\mathbb{R}$ and
$\Sigma=[\sigma^2]$.

Write $v=\sigma^2>0$.

By the assumptions of GDA,
\[
p(y^{(i)};\phi)
=
\phi^{y^{(i)}}(1-\phi)^{1-y^{(i)}},
\]
and
\[
p(x^{(i)}\mid y^{(i)})
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi v}}
\exp\left(
-\frac{
(x^{(i)}-\mu_{y^{(i)}})^2
}{2v}
\right).
\]

Since the training observations are independent,
the log-likelihood is
\[
\begin{aligned}
\ell(\phi,\mu_0,\mu_1,v)
={}&
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\log\left[
p(x^{(i)}\mid y^{(i)})
p(y^{(i)})
\right]
\\[4pt]
={}&
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\left[
y^{(i)}\log\phi
+
(1-y^{(i)})\log(1-\phi)
\right]
\\[4pt]
&-
\frac{m}{2}\log(2\pi)
-
\frac{m}{2}\log v
\\[4pt]
&-
\frac{1}{2v}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
(x^{(i)}-\mu_{y^{(i)}})^2.
\end{aligned}
\]

We maximize the log-likelihood with respect to
$\phi$, $\mu_0$, $\mu_1$, and $v$.

\medskip
\noindent\colorbox{blue!20}{\textbf{Estimation of $\phi$.}}

Define
\[
m_k
=
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\ind{y^{(i)}=k},
\qquad k\in\{0,1\},
\]
so that $m_0+m_1=m$.

The terms depending on $\phi$ are
\[
\ell_\phi
=
m_1\log\phi+m_0\log(1-\phi).
\]

Differentiating with respect to $\phi$,
\[
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\phi}
=
\frac{m_1}{\phi}
-
\frac{m_0}{1-\phi}.
\]

By the first-order optimality condition,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{m_1}{\phi}
-
\frac{m_0}{1-\phi}
&=0,
\\[4pt]
m_1(1-\phi)
&=m_0\phi,
\\[4pt]
m_1
&=(m_0+m_1)\phi
=m\phi.
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore,
\[
\boxed{
\hat{\phi}
=
\frac{m_1}{m}
=
\frac{1}{m}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\ind{y^{(i)}=1}.
}
\]

Moreover,
\[
\frac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial\phi^2}
=
-\frac{m_1}{\phi^2}
-
\frac{m_0}{(1-\phi)^2}
<0.
\]

Hence, the log-likelihood is strictly concave
in $\phi$, and the stationary point is its
unique global maximum.

\medskip
\noindent\colorbox{blue!20}{\textbf{Estimation of $\mu_0$ and $\mu_1$.}}

For $k\in\{0,1\}$, the terms depending on
$\mu_k$ are
\[
\ell_{\mu_k}
=
-\frac{1}{2v}
\sum_{i:y^{(i)}=k}
(x^{(i)}-\mu_k)^2.
\]

Differentiating with respect to $\mu_k$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mu_k}
&=
-\frac{1}{2v}
\sum_{i:y^{(i)}=k}
2(x^{(i)}-\mu_k)(-1)
\\[4pt]
&=
\frac{1}{v}
\sum_{i:y^{(i)}=k}
(x^{(i)}-\mu_k).
\end{aligned}
\]

Setting the derivative equal to zero,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i:y^{(i)}=k}
(x^{(i)}-\mu_k)
&=0,
\\[4pt]
\sum_{i:y^{(i)}=k}x^{(i)}
&=m_k\mu_k.
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus,
\[
\boxed{
\hat{\mu}_k
=
\frac{1}{m_k}
\sum_{i:y^{(i)}=k}x^{(i)}
=
\frac{
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\ind{y^{(i)}=k}x^{(i)}
}{
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\ind{y^{(i)}=k}
}.
}
\]

Furthermore,
\[
\frac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial\mu_k^2}
=
-\frac{m_k}{v}<0.
\]

Therefore, each stationary point is the
unique maximizer with respect to $\mu_k$.

Notice that the optimal mean parameters do
not depend on the value of $v$.

\medskip
\noindent\colorbox{blue!20}{\textbf{Estimation of $\sigma^2$.}}


Substituting the maximum likelihood estimates
of the mean parameters, define
\[
s
=
\frac{1}{m}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
(x^{(i)}-\hat{\mu}_{y^{(i)}})^2.
\]

The terms depending on $v$ become
\[
\begin{aligned}
\ell(v)
&=
-\frac{m}{2}\log v
-
\frac{1}{2v}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
(x^{(i)}-\hat{\mu}_{y^{(i)}})^2
+C
\\[4pt]
&=
-\frac{m}{2}
\left(
\log v+\frac{s}{v}
\right)+C,
\end{aligned}
\]
where $C$ is independent of $v$.

Differentiating,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\ell'(v)
&=
-\frac{m}{2}
\left(
\frac{1}{v}-\frac{s}{v^2}
\right)
\\[4pt]
&=
\frac{m(s-v)}{2v^2}.
\end{aligned}
\]

The first-order optimality condition gives
\[
\ell'(v)=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
v=s.
\]

Since $s>0$ and $v>0$,
\[
\ell'(v)
\begin{cases}
>0, & 0<v<s,\\
=0, & v=s,\\
<0, & v>s.
\end{cases}
\]

Hence, $v=s$ is the unique global maximizer.

Recalling that $v=\sigma^2$, we obtain
\[
\boxed{
\hat{\sigma}^2
=
\frac{1}{m}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
(x^{(i)}-\hat{\mu}_{y^{(i)}})^2.
}
\]
This completes part (i).

\medskip

\noindent\colorbox{yellow!20}{\textbf{(ii) Generalization to Arbitrary Dimensions.}}

Now suppose that $n$ is an arbitrary positive
integer, with
\[
x^{(i)},\mu_0,\mu_1\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
\qquad
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},
\qquad
\Sigma\succ0.
\]

Make a change of variable: define the precision matrix
\[
\boxed{
K=\Sigma^{-1}.
}
\]

Since $\Sigma$ is symmetric positive definite,
$K$ is also symmetric positive definite. Note that $K$ and $\Sigma^{-1}$ is bijective,
hence optimizing $l$ w.r.t. $\Sigma^{-1}$ is equivalent of optimizing l w.r.t. $K$

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{The Multivariate Log-Likelihood.}

Recall that
\[
p(x^{(i)}\mid y^{(i)})
=
\frac{1}{
(2\pi)^{n/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}
}
\exp\left(
-\frac{1}{2}
(x^{(i)}-\mu_{y^{(i)}})^T
\Sigma^{-1}
(x^{(i)}-\mu_{y^{(i)}})
\right).
\]

Taking logarithms and substituting
$K=\Sigma^{-1}$, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
\ell(\phi,\mu_0,\mu_1,K)
={}&
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\left[
y^{(i)}\log\phi
+
(1-y^{(i)})\log(1-\phi)
\right]
\\[4pt]
&-
\frac{mn}{2}\log(2\pi)
-
\frac{m}{2}\log\det\Sigma
\\[4pt]
&-
\frac{1}{2}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
(x^{(i)}-\mu_{y^{(i)}})^T
K
(x^{(i)}-\mu_{y^{(i)}}).
\end{aligned}
\]

Since
\[
\log\det\Sigma
=
-\log\det K,
\]
we may rewrite this as
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\ell(\phi,\mu_0,\mu_1,K)
={}&
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\left[
y^{(i)}\log\phi
+
(1-y^{(i)})\log(1-\phi)
\right]
\\[4pt]
&+
\frac{m}{2}\log\det K
\\[4pt]
&-
\frac{1}{2}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
(x^{(i)}-\mu_{y^{(i)}})^T
K
(x^{(i)}-\mu_{y^{(i)}})
+C,
\end{aligned}
}
\]
where $C=-\frac{mn}{2}\log(2\pi)$.

The terms involving $\phi$ are identical
to those in part (i). Therefore,
\[
\boxed{
\hat{\phi}
=
\frac{1}{m}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\ind{y^{(i)}=1}.
}
\]

\medskip
\noindent\colorbox{blue!20}{\textbf{Estimation of the Mean Vectors.}}

For $k\in\{0,1\}$, consider the terms
depending on $\mu_k$:
\[
\ell_{\mu_k}
=
-\frac{1}{2}
\sum_{i:y^{(i)}=k}
(x^{(i)}-\mu_k)^T
K
(x^{(i)}-\mu_k).
\]

Recall that for a symmetric matrix $A$,
\[
\nabla_u
\left[
(x-u)^TA(x-u)
\right]
=
2A(u-x).
\]

Since $K$ is symmetric,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\mu_k}\ell
&=
-\frac{1}{2}
\sum_{i:y^{(i)}=k}
2K(\mu_k-x^{(i)})
\\[4pt]
&=
K
\sum_{i:y^{(i)}=k}
(x^{(i)}-\mu_k)
\\[4pt]
&=
K
\left(
\sum_{i:y^{(i)}=k}x^{(i)}
-
m_k\mu_k
\right).
\end{aligned}
\]

Hence,
\[
\boxed{
\nabla_{\mu_k}\ell
=
K
\left(
\sum_{i:y^{(i)}=k}x^{(i)}
-
m_k\mu_k
\right).
}
\]

Applying the first-order optimality condition,
\[
K
\left(
\sum_{i:y^{(i)}=k}x^{(i)}
-
m_k\mu_k
\right)
=0.
\]

Since $K\succ0$, it is invertible.
Therefore,
\[
\sum_{i:y^{(i)}=k}x^{(i)}
-
m_k\mu_k
=0.
\]

Consequently,
\[
\boxed{
\hat{\mu}_k
=
\frac{
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\ind{y^{(i)}=k}x^{(i)}
}{
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\ind{y^{(i)}=k}
},
\qquad k\in\{0,1\}.
}
\]

Moreover, the Hessian with respect
to $\mu_k$ is
\[
\boxed{
\nabla_{\mu_k}^{2}\ell
=
-m_kK.
}
\]

Since $m_k>0$ and $K\succ0$,
\[
-m_kK\prec0.
\]

Thus, the log-likelihood is strictly concave
in $\mu_k$, and the stationary point is its
unique maximizer for every fixed $K\succ0$.

In particular, the maximum likelihood
estimators of the mean vectors are
independent of the feature dimension $n$.

\medskip
\noindent\colorbox{blue!20}{\textbf{Estimation of the Covariance Matrix.}}

Having obtained the maximum likelihood
estimates of the mean vectors, define
\[
r_i
=
x^{(i)}-\hat{\mu}_{y^{(i)}}
\]
and the scatter matrix
\[
\boxed{
S
=
\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_ir_i^T.
}
\]

The terms depending on $K$ are
\[
\ell(K)
=
\frac{m}{2}\log\det K
-
\frac{1}{2}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_i^TKr_i
+C.
\]

Since each quadratic form is a scalar,
we have
\[
\begin{aligned}
r_i^TKr_i
&=
\operatorname{tr}(r_i^TKr_i)
\\[4pt]
&=
\operatorname{tr}(Kr_ir_i^T),
\end{aligned}
\]
where the second equality follows
from the cyclic property of the trace.

Therefore,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_i^TKr_i
&=
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\operatorname{tr}(Kr_ir_i^T)
\\[4pt]
&=
\operatorname{tr}
\left(
K\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_ir_i^T
\right)
\\[4pt]
&=
\operatorname{tr}(KS).
\end{aligned}
\]

Hence,
\[
\boxed{
\ell(K)
=
\frac{m}{2}\log\det K
-
\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(KS)
+C.
}
\]

We now differentiate with respect to
the precision matrix $K$.

Recall the established matrix calculus identities
\[
\nabla_A\operatorname{tr}(AB)=B^T
\]
and
\[
\nabla_A\det A
=
\det(A)(A^{-1})^T.
\]

By the chain rule,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_A\log\det A
&=
\frac{1}{\det A}
\nabla_A\det A
\\[4pt]
&=
\frac{1}{\det A}
\det(A)(A^{-1})^T
\\[4pt]
&=
(A^{-1})^T.
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus,
\[
\boxed{
\nabla_A\log\det A
=
(A^{-1})^T.
}
\]

Applying these identities to the log-likelihood,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_K\ell
&=
\frac{m}{2}
\nabla_K\log\det K
-
\frac{1}{2}
\nabla_K\operatorname{tr}(KS)
\\[6pt]
&=
\frac{m}{2}(K^{-1})^T
-
\frac{1}{2}S^T.
\end{aligned}
\]

Since $K$ and $S$ are symmetric,
\[
(K^{-1})^T=K^{-1},
\qquad
S^T=S.
\]

Therefore,
\[
\boxed{
\nabla_K\ell
=
\frac{1}{2}
\left(
mK^{-1}-S
\right).
}
\]

Applying the first-order optimality condition,
\[
\nabla_K\ell=0,
\]
we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
mK^{-1}-S
&=0,
\\[4pt]
mK^{-1}
&=S,
\\[4pt]
K^{-1}
&=\frac{1}{m}S.
\end{aligned}
\]

Recalling that $K=\Sigma^{-1}$,
we conclude that
\[
\boxed{
\hat{\Sigma}
=
\frac{1}{m}S.
}
\]

Substituting the definition of $S$,
\[
\boxed{
\hat{\Sigma}
=
\frac{1}{m}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\left(
x^{(i)}-\hat{\mu}_{y^{(i)}}
\right)
\left(
x^{(i)}-\hat{\mu}_{y^{(i)}}
\right)^T.
}
\]

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Verification of Global Maximality.}

It remains to verify that the stationary point
is a global maximizer.

Recall that
\[
\ell(K)
=
\frac{m}{2}\log\det K
-
\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(KS)
+C.
\]

The function
\[
K\longmapsto\log\det K
\]
is strictly concave on the set of symmetric
positive definite matrices.

Furthermore,
\[
K\longmapsto\operatorname{tr}(KS)
\]
is linear in $K$.

Consequently, $\ell(K)$ is strictly concave
on the convex set of symmetric positive
definite matrices.

By assumption, $S\succ0$. Therefore,
\[
K^*
=
mS^{-1}
\]
exists and is positive definite.

Moreover,
\[
\nabla_K\ell(K^*)=0.
\]

Since $\ell$ is strictly concave,
this stationary point is its unique
global maximizer.

Hence,
\[
\boxed{
\hat{\Sigma}
=
(K^*)^{-1}
=
\frac{1}{m}S.
}
\]

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Conclusion.}

Combining the results of parts (i) and (ii),
the maximum likelihood estimators of the
GDA parameters are
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\phi}
&=
\frac{1}{m}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\ind{y^{(i)}=1},
\\[10pt]
\hat{\mu}_0
&=
\frac{
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\ind{y^{(i)}=0}x^{(i)}
}{
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\ind{y^{(i)}=0}
},
\\[10pt]
\hat{\mu}_1
&=
\frac{
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\ind{y^{(i)}=1}x^{(i)}
}{
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\ind{y^{(i)}=1}
},
\\[10pt]
\hat{\Sigma}
&=
\frac{1}{m}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
(x^{(i)}-\hat{\mu}_{y^{(i)}})
(x^{(i)}-\hat{\mu}_{y^{(i)}})^T.
\end{aligned}
}
\]

Thus, the maximum likelihood estimators derived
in the one-dimensional case extend to arbitrary
finite dimensions, provided that the empirical
residual covariance matrix is positive definite.

\hfill$\square$

\item \textbf{[30 points] Incomplete, Positive-Only Labels}

In this problem we will consider training binary classifiers in
situations where we do not have full access to the labels.

In particular, we consider a scenario, which is not too infrequent in
real life, where we have labels only for a subset of the positive
examples. All the negative examples and the rest of the positive
examples are unlabeled.

That is, we assume a dataset
\[
\left\{
(x^{(i)},t^{(i)},y^{(i)})
\right\}_{i=1}^{m},
\]
where
\[
t^{(i)}\in\{0,1\}
\]
is the ``true'' label, and where
\[
y^{(i)}
=
\begin{cases}
1, & x^{(i)}\text{ is labeled},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

All labeled examples are positive, which is to say
\[
p(t^{(i)}=1\mid y^{(i)}=1)=1,
\]
but unlabeled examples may be positive or negative.

Our goal in the problem is to construct a binary classifier $h$ of
the true label $t$, with only access to the partial labels $y$.

In other words, we want to construct $h$ such that
\[
h(x^{(i)})
\approx
p(t^{(i)}=1\mid x^{(i)})
\]
as closely as possible, using only $x$ and $y$.

\textit{Real world example:}
Suppose we maintain a database of proteins which are involved in
transmitting signals across membranes. Every example added to the
database is involved in a signaling process, but there are many
proteins involved in cross-membrane signaling which are missing from
the database.

It would be useful to train a classifier to identify proteins that
should be added to the database.

In our notation, each example $x^{(i)}$ corresponds to a protein,
$y^{(i)}=1$ if the protein is in the database and $0$ otherwise, and
$t^{(i)}=1$ if the protein is involved in a cross-membrane signaling
process and thus should be added to the database, and $0$ otherwise.

\item \textbf{[5 points]}

Suppose that each $y^{(i)}$ and $x^{(i)}$ are conditionally
independent given $t^{(i)}$:
\[
p(y^{(i)}=1\mid t^{(i)}=1,x^{(i)})
=
p(y^{(i)}=1\mid t^{(i)}=1).
\]

Note this is equivalent to saying that labeled examples were selected
uniformly at random from the set of positive examples.

Prove that the probability of an example being labeled differs by a
constant factor from the probability of an example being positive.

That is, show that
\[
p(t^{(i)}=1\mid x^{(i)})
=
\frac{
p(y^{(i)}=1\mid x^{(i)})
}{\alpha}
\]
for some
\[
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}.
\]

% Solution to 2(a) will go here

\medskip
\noindent\colorbox{yellow}{\textbf{Solution.}}

\vspace{3mm}

Let's first briefly review the concept of Conditional Chain Rule.

Let $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb P)$ be a probability space,
and let $A,B,C\in\mathcal F$ be events such that
$\mathbb P(B\cap C)>0$. Then
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb P(A\cap B\mid C)
=
\mathbb P(A\mid B\cap C)\,
\mathbb P(B\mid C).
}
\]
The notation $\mathbb P(A\mid B,C)$ is shorthand for
$\mathbb P(A\mid B\cap C)$; the comma denotes simultaneous
conditioning on both events.

\paragraph{Theorem (Conditional Law of Total Probability).}
Let $\{B_i\}_{i=1}^{n}$ be a finite measurable partition
of $\Omega$(total space), and let $C\in\mathcal F$ satisfy
$\mathbb P(C)>0$. Then, for any event $A\in\mathcal F$,
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb P(A\mid C)
=
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\mathbb P(A\mid B_i\cap C)\,
\mathbb P(B_i\mid C).
}
\]
Terms with $\mathbb P(B_i\cap C)=0$ are understood to
contribute zero to the sum.

\paragraph{Proof.}
Since $\{B_i\}_{i=1}^{n}$ partitions $\Omega$,
the events $\{A\cap B_i\cap C\}_{i=1}^{n}$
are pairwise disjoint, and
\[
A\cap C
=
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}(A\cap B_i\cap C).
\]
Consequently,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(A\mid C)
&=\frac{\mathbb P(A\cap C)}{\mathbb P(C)}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\frac{\mathbb P(A\cap B_i\cap C)}{\mathbb P(C)}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\underbrace{
\frac{\mathbb P(A\cap B_i\cap C)}
     {\mathbb P(B_i\cap C)}
}_{\mathbb P(A\mid B_i\cap C)}
\underbrace{
\frac{\mathbb P(B_i\cap C)}
     {\mathbb P(C)}
}_{\mathbb P(B_i\mid C)}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\mathbb P(A\mid B_i\cap C)\,
\mathbb P(B_i\mid C).
\end{aligned}
\]
\hfill $\square$

\vspace{3mm}

Now back to the original problem. We are motivated to inspect the relationship between 
$p(t^{(i)} | x^{(i)})$ and $p(y^{(i)} = 1 | x^{(i)})$.

We first formalize these variables. Let $X$ be a feature vector, $T\in\{0,1\}$ a latent
binary label, and $Y\in\{0,1\}$ an observed label. Conditioning on $X=x$ and partitioning according to
the possible values of $T$, we obtain
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(Y=1\mid X=x)
={}&\mathbb P(Y=1\mid T=1,X=x)
    \mathbb P(T=1\mid X=x)\\
&+\mathbb P(Y=1\mid T=0,X=x)
    \mathbb P(T=0\mid X=x).
\end{aligned}
}
\]

For continuous $X$, this identity is interpreted
using regular conditional probabilities rather than
conditioning directly on the zero-probability event
$\{X=x\}$. It holds for almost every $x$ with respect
to the distribution of $X$.

We know that negative examples are never labeled:
\[
\mathbb P(Y=1\mid T=0,X=x)=0.
\]
Furthermore, we know that labeling probability
among true positive examples is independent of $X$:
\[
\mathbb P(Y=1\mid T=1,X=x)
=
\mathbb P(Y=1\mid T=1)
=:\alpha.
\]
Then the conditional law of total probability gives
\[
\mathbb P(Y=1\mid X=x)
=
\alpha\,\mathbb P(T=1\mid X=x).
\]
Therefore, provided $\alpha>0$,
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb P(T=1\mid X=x)
=
\frac{\mathbb P(Y=1\mid X=x)}{\alpha}.
}
\]

\item \textbf{[5 points]}

Consider the Poisson distribution parameterized by $\lambda$:
\[
p(y;\lambda)
=
\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^y}{y!}.
\]

Show that the Poisson distribution is in the exponential family, and
clearly state the values for
\[
b(y),\qquad
\eta,\qquad
T(y),\qquad
a(\eta).
\]

% Solution to 3(a) will go here.

\medskip
\noindent\colorbox{yellow}{\textbf{Solution.}}
\vspace{3mm}

Rewriting the probability mass function, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
p(y;\lambda)
&=\exp\left(
\ln\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^y}{y!}
\right)\\
&=\exp\left(
-\lambda+y\ln\lambda-\ln(y!)
\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{y!}\exp\left(y\ln\lambda-\lambda\right).
\end{aligned}
\]

Define the natural parameter
\[
\eta=\ln\lambda
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\lambda=e^\eta.
\]

Substituting this into the expression above gives
\[
p(y;\eta)
=
\frac{1}{y!}\exp\left(\eta y-e^\eta\right).
\]

Comparing with the exponential family representation, we identify
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
b(y)&=\frac{1}{y!},\\[4pt]
\eta&=\ln\lambda,\\[4pt]
T(y)&=y,\\[4pt]
a(\eta)&=e^\eta.
\end{aligned}
}
\]

Therefore, the Poisson distribution belongs to the exponential family.
\hfill $\square$

\item \textbf{[15 points] Convexity of Generalized Linear Models}

In this question we will explore and show some nice properties of
Generalized Linear Models, specifically those related to their use of
Exponential Family distributions to model the output.

Most commonly, GLMs are trained by using the negative log-likelihood
(NLL) as the loss function. This is mathematically equivalent to
Maximum Likelihood Estimation; maximizing the log-likelihood is
equivalent to minimizing the negative log-likelihood.

In this problem, our goal is to show that the NLL loss of a GLM is a
convex function with respect to the model parameters.

As a reminder, this is convenient because a convex function is one
for which any local minimum is also a global minimum.

To recap, an exponential family distribution is one whose probability
density can be represented as
\[
p(y;\eta)
=
b(y)
\exp\left(
\eta^TT(y)-a(\eta)
\right),
\]
where $\eta$ is the natural parameter of the distribution.

Moreover, in a Generalized Linear Model, $\eta$ is modeled as
\[
\theta^Tx,
\]
where
\[
x\in\mathbb{R}^n
\]
are the input features of the example, and
\[
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^n
\]
are learnable parameters.

In order to show that the NLL loss is convex for GLMs, we break down
the process into sub-parts, and approach them one at a time.

Our approach is to show that the second derivative (i.e.\ Hessian) of
the loss with respect to the model parameters is Positive
Semi-Definite (PSD) at all values of the model parameters.

We will also show some nice properties of Exponential Family
distributions as intermediate steps.

For the sake of convenience we restrict ourselves to the case where
$\eta$ is a scalar.

Assume
\[
p(Y\mid X;\theta)
\sim
\operatorname{ExponentialFamily}(\eta),
\]
where
\[
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
\]
is a scalar, and
\[
T(y)=y.
\]

This makes the exponential family representation take the form
\[
p(y;\eta)
=
b(y)\exp(\eta y-a(\eta)).
\]

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]

% ------------------------------------------------------------
% 4(a)
% ------------------------------------------------------------

\item \textbf{[5 points]}

Derive an expression for the mean of the distribution.

Show that
\[
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X;\theta]
\]
can be represented as the gradient of the log-partition function
$a$ with respect to the natural parameter $\eta$.

\textit{Hint:}
Start with observing that
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}
\int p(y;\eta)\,dy
=
\int
\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}
p(y;\eta)\,dy.
\]

% Solution to 4(a) will go here.

\medskip
\noindent\colorbox{yellow}{\textbf{Solution.}}
\vspace{3mm}


By the definition of conditional expectation,
\[
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x;\theta]
=
\int_{\mathcal Y} y\,p(y;\eta)\,dy.
\]

First, differentiating $p(y;\eta)$ with respect to $\eta$, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}p(y;\eta)
&=
b(y)\exp\left(\eta y-a(\eta)\right)
\left(y-\frac{\partial a}{\partial\eta}\right)\\
&=
p(y;\eta)\left(y-\frac{\partial a}{\partial\eta}\right)\\
&=
y\,p(y;\eta)
-
\frac{\partial a}{\partial\eta}p(y;\eta).
\end{aligned}
\]

Rearranging,
\[
\boxed{
y\,p(y;\eta)
=
\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}p(y;\eta)
+
\frac{\partial a}{\partial\eta}p(y;\eta).
}
\]

Substituting into the expression for the conditional expectation,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x;\theta]
&=
\int_{\mathcal Y} y\,p(y;\eta)\,dy\\[4pt]
&=
\int_{\mathcal Y}
\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}p(y;\eta)\,dy
+
\frac{\partial a}{\partial\eta}
\int_{\mathcal Y}p(y;\eta)\,dy\\[4pt]
&=
\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}
\int_{\mathcal Y}p(y;\eta)\,dy
+
\frac{\partial a}{\partial\eta}
\int_{\mathcal Y}p(y;\eta)\,dy\\[4pt]
&=
\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}(1)
+
\frac{\partial a}{\partial\eta}\cdot 1\\[4pt]
&=
\frac{\partial a}{\partial\eta}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Here, differentiation under the integral sign is justified
by the regularity conditions of the exponential family,
and we have used the normalization property
\[
\int_{\mathcal Y}p(y;\eta)\,dy=1.
\]

Therefore,
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x;\theta]
=
a'(\eta)
=
a'(\theta^T x).
}
\]

Hence, the conditional mean is given by the first derivative
of the log-partition function with respect to the natural parameter.
\hfill $\square$

% ------------------------------------------------------------
% 4(b)
% ------------------------------------------------------------

\item \textbf{[5 points]}

Next, derive an expression for the variance of the distribution.

In particular, show that
\[
\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X;\theta)
\]
can be expressed as the derivative of the mean with respect to $\eta$,
i.e.\ the second derivative of the log-partition function
$a(\eta)$ with respect to the natural parameter $\eta$.

% Solution to 4(b) will go here.

\medskip
\noindent\colorbox{yellow}{\textbf{Solution.}}
\vspace{3mm}

Recall that the exponential family distribution with $T(y)=y$ is
given by
\[
p(y;\eta)=b(y)\exp\left(\eta y-a(\eta)\right),
\qquad \eta=\theta^T x.
\]

By the definition of conditional variance,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=x;\theta)
&=\mathbb{E}\left[
\left(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x;\theta]\right)^2
\mid X=x;\theta
\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[Y^2\mid X=x;\theta]
-\left(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x;\theta]\right)^2.
\end{aligned}
\]

From part (a), we have established that
\[
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x;\theta]=a'(\eta).
\]

Thus, it suffices to show that
\[
a''(\eta)
=
\mathbb{E}[Y^2\mid X=x;\theta]
-\left(a'(\eta)\right)^2.
\]

Differentiating the result from part (a) with respect to
the natural parameter $\eta$, we obtain
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x;\theta]
=
a''(\eta).
\]

On the other hand, by the definition of conditional expectation,
\[
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x;\theta]
=
\int_{\mathcal Y}y\,p(y;\eta)\,dy.
\]

Assuming the regularity conditions that justify differentiation
under the integral sign,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x;\theta]
&=
\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}
\int_{\mathcal Y}y\,p(y;\eta)\,dy\\
&=
\int_{\mathcal Y}
\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}
\left(y\,p(y;\eta)\right)\,dy.
\end{aligned}
\]

Using the exponential family representation,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}
\left(y\,p(y;\eta)\right)
&=
\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}
\left(y\,b(y)e^{\eta y-a(\eta)}\right)\\
&=
y\,b(y)e^{\eta y-a(\eta)}
\left(y-a'(\eta)\right)\\
&=
y^2p(y;\eta)-a'(\eta)y\,p(y;\eta).
\end{aligned}
\]

Substituting this expression into the integral yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
a''(\eta)
&=
\int_{\mathcal Y}
\left(
y^2p(y;\eta)-a'(\eta)y\,p(y;\eta)
\right)\,dy\\[4pt]
&=
\int_{\mathcal Y}y^2p(y;\eta)\,dy
-a'(\eta)\int_{\mathcal Y}y\,p(y;\eta)\,dy\\[4pt]
&=
\mathbb{E}[Y^2\mid X=x;\theta]
-a'(\eta)\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x;\theta]\\[4pt]
&=
\mathbb{E}[Y^2\mid X=x;\theta]
-\left(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x;\theta]\right)^2\\[4pt]
&=
\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=x;\theta).
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore,
\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=x;\theta)
=
\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x;\theta]
=
\frac{\partial^2a(\eta)}{\partial\eta^2}
=
a''(\theta^Tx).
}
\]

Hence, the conditional variance is equal to the second derivative
of the log-partition function with respect to the natural parameter.
\hfill $\square$
